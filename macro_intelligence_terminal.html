📉 INDIA OIS & MIBOR — WHAT THE MARKET PRICES FOR THE RBI
This is India's version of "Fed Funds futures" — the MIBOR-OIS curve prices exactly what the market expects the RBI to do. MIBOR (Mumbai Interbank Offered Rate) is the overnight benchmark; the OIS (Overnight Indexed Swap) curve built on it reveals the priced path of rate cuts. Reading this curve tells you whether the bond market agrees with our regime call — and whether rate-sensitive sectors (banks, NBFCs, autos, real estate) have a tailwind coming.
1 · The Policy Corridor & Benchmarks (latest)
📐 "Isn't MIBOR around 7.5%?" — Why it structurally can't be, in June 2026
A common reflex is to remember MIBOR near 7%+. That was true in 2023–24, when the repo rate was 6.50%. But the RBI has cut three times since (Dec 2025 and Feb 2026 among them), and the repo is now 5.25% (held at the June 3–5 MPC, neutral stance). MIBOR moves with the repo, so it has come down too.

The hard ceiling: overnight MIBOR is the rate banks lend each other unsecured, and it is boxed inside the RBI's policy corridor:
  MSF (ceiling) ........ 5.50% ← no bank borrows interbank above this; it'd use RBI instead
  Repo rate ........... 5.25% ← the policy anchor
  SDF (floor) ......... 5.00% ← no bank lends below this; it'd park at RBI instead
  Overnight MIBOR ..... ~5.3–5.9% (sits just around the corridor)
A 7.5% overnight MIBOR would be 200 bps above the MSF ceiling — impossible, because any bank could just borrow from the RBI at 5.50% instead of paying 7.50% to another bank. The corridor is a hard cap.

What about the 6.63% in this terminal? That's the 3-month TERM MIBOR (Trading Economics, Jun 18) — a 3-month rate, which sits above overnight because it carries term premium + bank credit spread. Overnight ≈ 5.85%, 3-month ≈ 6.63%. Both are correct and internally consistent. Neither is anywhere near 7.5%.

Bottom line: with repo at 5.25%, MIBOR in the 5.3% (overnight) to 6.6% (3-month) range is exactly where it should be. 7.5% would require a repo rate near 7% — which India hasn't seen since 2018, or briefly in the 2023 tightening peak (repo 6.50%, term MIBOR ~7%). The number isn't wrong; it's just lower than it was two years ago because the RBI has eased.

📡 What Interest Rates Are Telling You
2 · The MIBOR-OIS Curve — Priced RBI Path
How to read it: if the OIS curve slopes down (longer tenors below the overnight rate), the market is pricing rate cuts. The gap between the 1-year OIS and the current repo tells you how many cuts are priced. A downward MIBOR-OIS curve = bond market expects easing = tailwind for duration and rate-sensitive equities.

Key insight (RBI's own research, Ghosh & Acharya): India's OIS rate is driven by the G-sec rate, the call rate, and crucially liquidity conditions (the call−repo spread) — not contemporaneous inflation. A rising MIBOR signals tight liquidity; a falling MIBOR signals surplus. Right now liquidity is balanced-to-surplus and the curve leans toward easing, consistent with our Goldilocks call.
4 · What It Means for Positioning
5 · OIS Rates Table & the Swap Spread (OIS vs G-Sec)
The OIS rate is the market's prediction of the average overnight rate over each tenor — not the current repo. The OIS curve is the "cleaner" risk-free curve; the G-Sec curve carries supply/demand noise. Their difference is the swap spread, which tells you about bond supply pressure and term premium.

What the swap spread says: the spread widening with tenor (+14bp at 1Y → +85bp at 10Y) is the bond market demanding extra yield for India's heavy G-Sec issuance and duration risk at the long end — even as the OIS curve (pure rate expectations) prices cuts. The signal: the front-end OIS pricing easing is the cleaner read on RBI policy; the wide long-end swap spread is fiscal supply, not a changed rate view. For a trader: the OIS front-end is where to read RBI intent; the G-Sec long end is where supply/demand noise lives. (Per the OIS-vs-bond-curve distinction: OIS is the "truer" risk-free expectation curve, free of supply distortions.)

💼 MCX Trading Cost Quick Reference
Margin requirements (~): Gold 5%, Silver 8%, Crude/NatGas 14% (incl. 10% additional risk margin), Base metals 6-10%.
Tax efficiency: SGB (Sovereign Gold Bonds) — 0% tax on capital gains if held to maturity (8 years) + 2.5% annual coupon. Best gold exposure for Indian residents.
STT: Commodity STT 0.01% on sell-side futures (lower than equities 0.05%). Long bias gets cheaper execution.
Settlement: Energy & base metals = T+1 cash settled. Bullion = optional physical delivery for Gold 1kg / Silver 30kg lots.

📖 What This Means for the Trader
$672.6bn sounds huge. But the drawdown rate matters more. RBI sold ~$40bn over 11 weeks (~$3.6bn/week average). At current Brent-driven pace, reserves cross below $670bn in another 5 weeks. That's the level where credit rating agencies start watching closely.

Why the gold-import compression matters. The PM publicly asked Indians to cut foreign travel + stop gold imports for a year. This is unprecedented — last seen in 2013 BoP crisis. Tells you the reserve drain is being taken seriously at the top.

Read-through to the regime model. Falling reserves = RBI has less ammo to defend INR = INR likely weakens further = imported inflation rises = CPI buffer narrows = Reflation tilt increases. The forex chart is a leading indicator for the CPI you'll see in 4-6 weeks.

Action triggers: If reserves fall below $680bn, RBI likely stops INR defense and lets it depreciate (raises the regime stagflation tilt). If reserves stabilize back above $700bn, INR pressure recedes (Goldilocks strengthens). Reserves at $672.6bn, 3rd straight weekly rise — pressure easing.

India at Mid-2026: A Goldilocks Economy on a Knife's Edge
An economist's read of where the Indian economy actually stands — June 2026

There is a particular kind of macroeconomic moment that is easy to misread: when the headline numbers look unambiguously good, but the balance of risks underneath them is more finely poised than the surface suggests. India in the middle of 2026 is exactly that moment. The economy is growing faster than almost any major peer, inflation is contained, oil has collapsed in India's favour, and equity markets sit near record highs. And yet the single most important macroeconomic variable of the next quarter — whether June inflation prints above or below 4% — is close enough to a coin-flip that the entire policy and market trajectory hinges on it.

This piece lays out where the economy stands, what the verified data says, and why the right characterisation is not "all clear" but rather "constructive, conditional, and worth watching closely."

The growth picture is genuinely strong — and that is not in dispute

Start with the unambiguous good news. India's GDP grew 7.8% in the fourth quarter of FY26 (January–March 2026), comfortably beating the 7.0–7.3% that economists had penciled in, and capping a full fiscal year of roughly 7.7% growth. That keeps India the fastest-growing major economy in the world, and — importantly — the composition of that growth is healthy rather than borrowed. The expansion was led by private consumption and fixed investment, with manufacturing posting double-digit gross-value-added growth and services (trade, transport, finance, IT) carrying their usual weight.

This matters because not all 7% growth is created equal. Growth driven by government spending alone is fragile; growth driven by private consumption and capital formation is self-sustaining. The FY26 print is the latter kind. The manufacturing strength in particular — helped by capacity utilisation running high and the Make-in-India industrial push — suggests the investment cycle that India has promised for a decade may finally be turning in earnest.

So the growth leg of the story is real. The complications lie elsewhere.

Inflation: contained, but with almost no margin for error

Retail inflation rose to 3.9% in May 2026, up from 3.5% in April. On its face, this is fine — it sits below the Reserve Bank of India's 4% target midpoint, which is why the central bank felt comfortable holding rates. But the direction and the proximity to the line are what an economist actually watches, and both are slightly uncomfortable. Inflation is rising toward the target rather than falling away from it, and the buffer to the 4% threshold has narrowed to roughly seven hundredths of a percentage point. That is not a margin; that is a rounding error.

Here is where the most important recent development enters the story, and it is a genuinely favourable one. Brent crude collapsed to around $72 a barrel in June, down roughly 12% on the month, after a de-escalation of Middle East tensions (a ceasefire and renewed diplomacy reopened the supply picture and drained the war-risk premium). For an economy that imports approximately 85% of its crude, this is close to the best possible exogenous gift. Cheaper oil feeds through — with a lag of about six weeks — into lower transport, manufacturing input, and ultimately retail prices. It eases the import bill, narrows the current-account deficit, and takes pressure off the rupee, all at once.

The honest analytical point is this: the oil collapse is disinflationary, and the market has been slow to fully price it. If June CPI (due around July 12) comes in soft because cheap oil is doing its work, the inflation worry largely dissolves and the RBI's path to rate cuts reopens. If it does not — if food or core stickiness offsets the oil relief — then India is genuinely at the boundary of a less benign regime. This is why the July inflation print is the most consequential single data release on the calendar.

The Reserve Bank is walking a deliberate tightrope

The RBI's June decision was telling in its caution. It held the repo rate at 5.25% with a neutral stance, but the accompanying forecasts revealed the central bank's discomfort: it trimmed its GDP growth forecast for the coming year to 6.6% and nudged up its inflation projections. In other words, even as the current data looks strong, the RBI is signalling that it sees the risks tilting toward slower growth and stickier prices ahead — a classic late-expansion central-bank posture.

What is more revealing still is what the RBI has done on the capital side, because it betrays where the real anxiety lies. Through 2026, foreign investors pulled roughly $13.7 billion out of Indian equities, and the rupee slid to record lows near ₹94–95 against the dollar earlier in the year. The central bank's response has been an unusually aggressive set of measures to court foreign capital back: exempting foreign institutional investors from capital-gains tax from April 2026, opening 15-, 30-, and 40-year government bonds to the Fully Accessible Route (which qualifies them for inclusion in global bond indices), and offering concessional forex-swap and hedging facilities to encourage external borrowing.

These are not the actions of a central bank that feels relaxed about its external position. They are the actions of one that recognises India's growth ambitions require foreign capital, and that the combination of a strong dollar, elevated US rates, and portfolio outflows had put the rupee and the reserves under real strain. The reserves themselves tell the story in miniature: they fell to a one-year low near $671 billion in May as the RBI sold dollars to defend the currency, then recovered to about $672.6 billion by mid-June as the pressure eased — a third consecutive weekly rise, helped by the very oil-and-geopolitics relief described above.

The currency and the carry: India's quiet advantage

One structural feature deserves emphasis because it is doing a lot of invisible work: India's real interest rate advantage. With the 10-year government bond yielding about 6.84% against inflation of 3.9%, India offers a real yield of roughly +2.9%. The United States, by contrast, with the Federal Reserve having cut to 3.50–3.75% against US inflation above 4%, offers a real policy rate that is barely positive or slightly negative.

That gap — close to three percentage points of real yield in India's favour — is the cushion underneath the rupee. It is why, even amid foreign equity outflows, the currency has stabilised rather than collapsed, and why foreign money is being tempted into Indian bonds even as it exits equities. For an economist, this real-rate differential is the single most reliable support for the external account, and it is one of the genuine strengths of India's current configuration. It is also why the bond-market opening to foreign investors is well-timed: India is paying a real return that most of the developed world cannot match.

The yield curve is sending an optimistic signal

A final technical point that the broader commentary often misses: India's yield curve is bull-steepening — short-term rates are falling faster than long-term rates, as the market begins to price RBI rate cuts. This is the constructive kind of curve move (as opposed to a "bear steepening" driven by fiscal or inflation fears, which is what the United States is wrestling with). A bull-steepening curve in a country with contained inflation and strong growth is about as benign a fixed-income signal as exists. It says the market expects easier policy ahead, growth to hold, and no imminent stress. India's curve and America's curve currently tell opposite stories — India's reflects a central bank with room to ease; America's reflects fiscal and term-premium anxiety.

So what is the honest verdict?

India sits in what economists call a "Goldilocks" configuration: growth above trend and accelerating, inflation below target and — thanks to the oil collapse — likely decelerating. By the standard two-axis framework of growth versus inflation, that is the most favourable of the four possible regimes, and both rule-based and machine-learned models put a high probability on it.

But the characterisation that does justice to the data is not "Goldilocks, all clear." It is "Goldilocks, conditional on the next inflation print, and supported by an oil move that could reverse." The economy has three genuine strengths — strong and well-composed growth, a powerful real-yield cushion under the currency, and a disinflationary oil tailwind — and two genuine vulnerabilities: an inflation rate sitting a rounding error below the line, and an external account still dependent on foreign capital that has been flighty.

The single most important thing to watch is the July inflation release. If cheap oil pulls it down, India's path is clear through the rest of 2026: rate cuts become possible, the rupee firms, the investment cycle extends, and the Goldilocks call is vindicated. If it surprises to the upside, India tips toward a "reflation" regime where the RBI's hands are tied and the calculus for both bonds and equities changes materially.

That is the knife's edge. The probabilities favour the benign outcome — the oil collapse is a strong disinflationary force and the growth data is robust — but a careful economist holds the conditionality firmly in view rather than declaring victory. India in mid-2026 is a strong economy in a genuinely good position, with one important number still to land.

The AI Compression Trade — What It Means for India's Sectors
An addendum applying the "obsolescence tax" framework (Capital Flows Research, with Alfie Kerswell) to Indian equities — June 2026

There is a framework gaining traction in global macro that reframes AI not as a technology theme but as a monetary-equivalent force. The argument: in 2021, negative real interest rates punished anyone holding cash, forcing capital into equities. In 2026, AI plays the same role through a different mechanism — an "obsolescence tax." If a company or sector is not integrating AI, its capital is being devalued in real terms faster than inflation ever did, because the compounding rate of AI capability now exceeds the rate at which cash was punished four years ago. The cleanest expression globally has been the spread between AI-beneficiaries and AI-at-risk names. The question worth asking — which India commentary has largely ignored — is where Indian sectors fall on that spread.

India's most AI-exposed sector cuts both ways

India's IT services sector is the single most AI-exposed part of the index, and the exposure is double-edged. The traditional model — labour arbitrage, billing thousands of engineers to write code and run processes — is precisely what AI compresses. When a six-month software build takes three weeks, the value of selling six months of engineer-hours collapses. This is not a cyclical headwind; it is a structural one, and it is the deeper reason behind the terminal's underweight on IT that goes beyond the rupee and the global-tech rout. The market is beginning to price the devaluation of capital tied to the old delivery model.

But the same sector could be a beneficiary if it moves up the stack — selling AI integration rather than headcount. The Indian IT majors that successfully reposition as the "picks-and-shovels" layer for enterprises adopting AI (the role Palantir plays in the US) would flip from at-risk to beneficiary. The spread within Indian IT — between firms genuinely rebuilding their production model around AI and those defending the old one — is likely to widen sharply. The terminal's blanket IT-underweight is the right cyclical call, but the structural story is a dispersion trade: short the labour-arbitrage model, watch for the repositioners.

Where India is structurally insulated — and where it benefits

The sectors the terminal currently favours are, not coincidentally, the ones least exposed to AI disruption and in several cases beneficiaries of it. Banking is an AI-adopter, not an AI-victim — AI compresses its cost base (fraud detection, underwriting, servicing) while its core deposit-and-lending franchise is regulatorily moated and physical. Capital goods and infrastructure build the physical layer — data centres, power, grid — that the AI buildout requires; India's ₹22 lakh crore infrastructure pipeline intersects directly with the global data-centre capex wave. Power and utilities are the unglamorous bottleneck of the entire AI cycle — compute needs electricity, and India's power-demand story gets a structural bid from it. Pharma and consumer are largely orthogonal to AI disruption — demand for medicine and staples does not compress when software gets cheaper.

The statecraft dimension — why this is also geopolitics

The framework's final move is to treat AI as economic statecraft: every country now needs its own AI production stack, which transmits directly into geopolitical risk and capital flows. For India this is both opportunity and pressure. India has the talent pool and the policy ambition (the IndiaAI mission, sovereign compute initiatives) to build a domestic stack — and doing so would create an entirely new beneficiary category in the index. But it also means India is now a participant in the semiconductor-and-compute arms race, exposed to the same TSMC chokepoint and supply-chain fragility that the global framework identifies as the key transmission channel. The India-US trade negotiations, the FII flow dynamics, and the rupee all sit downstream of how this plays out.

The synthesis for India: the AI-compression lens does not contradict the terminal's regime call — it deepens it. The Goldilocks-with-cheap-oil setup is the cyclical story; the AI obsolescence tax is the structural overlay running underneath it. Both point the same way on sector selection — own the physical-layer builders, the AI-adopters, and the insulated defensives; underweight the labour-arbitrage model that AI is quietly dismantling. The cyclical and structural signals rhyme, which is exactly when a sector view carries the most conviction.
