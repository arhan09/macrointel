📉 INDIA RATES — OIS, MIBOR AND THE LIVE TERM STRUCTURE: WHAT THE MARKET PRICES FOR THE RBI
This is India's version of "Fed Funds futures". Three curves live here, each labelled by how fresh it is: the policy corridor (SDF–repo–MSF, live), the T-bill and G-sec term structure off the RBI home page (official, daily, as-on-dated — the closest thing to a live priced policy path this runner can reach), and the MIBOR-OIS swap curve (FBIL's fixings, read from a keyless mirror and dated on the chart; overnight daily). The RBI has held the repo at — , the latest minutes, and the swap curve is read against it below. Reading these curves tells you whether the bond market agrees with the regime call on the first tab — and whether rate-sensitive sectors (banks, NBFCs, autos, real estate) face a headwind or a tailwind.
1 · The Policy Corridor & Benchmarks (latest)
📐 How to read this tab when the RBI is on hold and the next move could be up
Overnight is boxed by the corridor. Call money and MIBOR trade between the SDF floor and the MSF ceiling — repo ±—bp — and cannot leave it: any bank could deal with the RBI instead. So the overnight rate (~5.02% against a — repo) tells you about liquidity, not about the policy path. Below the repo = surplus cash; at the ceiling = stress. Section 1.5 scores it daily.

The path is in the term structure. A 91-day bill sitting on the repo says nothing is priced inside the quarter; a 364-day bill well above it says the market expects a higher average policy rate over the year (part term premium — the panel in 1.2 does the arithmetic and labels the bound). The dated G-secs beyond a year add the term and fiscal-supply premium on top; the swap spread (bond − OIS) at each tenor separates the two.

The swap curve is the cleanest expectations read. The MIBOR-OIS fixings (FBIL's benchmark, 1M to 5Y) are read every pass from a keyless mirror and printed with their own date — the mirror's free view lags the fixing by a few sessions, so the date is on the chart, not assumed. The overnight point is the daily MMO call. The page never asserts "X hikes priced" off one curve: the official bill curve in 1.2 is printed beside the swap and the two are checked against each other for direction and level.

Trader translation: hold-to-hike with surplus liquidity is the regime where duration is a headwind (long-gilt funds, long-bond proxies), floating-rate lenders with cheap deposits are neutral-to-positive (large private banks), and wholesale-funded NBFCs, autos and realty lose the rate tailwind of the easing cycle. The next MPC and the next CPI print are the two dates that re-price everything on this tab.

How to read it: the OIS rate at each tenor is the market's average expected overnight rate over that tenor. What this number actually is. The RBI's stock of foreign-currency assets, gold, SDRs and the IMF position — the buffer that lets a managed-float currency be managed. It is not a savings account; it is ammunition: sold to slow rupee depreciation, accumulated when flows are friendly.

How to read the moves. Reserves rising = the RBI is buying dollars (absorbing inflows, keeping the rupee competitive). Reserves falling = the RBI is defending — spending the buffer to smooth depreciation, or valuation effects (a stronger dollar and softer gold mark the non-dollar assets down without a single intervention). Fast drawdowns during INR pressure are the market's best evidence of how hard the RBI is leaning.

Why traders care. The buffer sets how long the RBI can keep USD/INR orderly. A fat buffer means step-moves are chosen, not forced; a thinning one means the currency, rates or both must eventually do the adjusting — which is a regime input, not a footnote.

💵 THE DOLLAR — how it's valued, and why India cares
There is no fair-value anchor for the dollar in the short run — only regimes. Three lenses do most of the work:

1 · Rate & growth differentials. Capital chases the highest risk-adjusted real yield. When US real rates and growth outrun the rest of G10, the dollar gets bid regardless of valuation. But differentials can decouple for months when fiscal or political risk premia take over — which is exactly the information: when the dollar falls despite a yield advantage, the market is charging the US a risk premium.

2 · The dollar smile. The dollar strengthens at both extremes — exceptional US growth (money chases US assets) and global crisis (money hides in the reserve currency). It weakens in the middle: mediocre US growth with the rest of the world improving. Placing today on the smile tells you more than any single indicator.

3 · The structural ledger. Twin deficits argue for a weaker dollar over decades; reserve-currency privilege (invoicing, Treasury demand, network effects) argues it erodes only slowly. Central-bank gold accumulation is the visible hedge against that erosion — diversification is real, but glacial. Both things are true; the trade is in the pace.

₹ THE RUPEE PLAYBOOK — a living primer (auto-updating)
How the rupee is actually valued — regime, anchors, behavior, and the trading checklist. The framing is stable; every figure refreshes with the data pipeline and the browser-live layer.
1 · What kind of currency is this? A managed float. Of the impossible trinity — fixed rate, open capital account, independent monetary policy — India keeps policy independence, manages the capital account, and lets the RBI smooth (not fix) the rate. Practical consequence: USD/INR trends are low-volatility with fat tails — long quiet drifts punctuated by step moves when smoothing pauses. Realized vol says when the regime is shifting: ….

2 · The structural anchors.
• Inflation differential (the PPP drift). India runs CPI (·%) above US trend inflation, so the rupee's fair path depreciates a few percent a year even when nothing is wrong. Flat USD/INR through time is real appreciation, not stability.
• Rate differential & carry. Repo ·% vs US rates leaves a carry cushion — IN 10Y ≈ ·% vs US 10Y ·. When that spread compresses, hedged flows thin out and the drift accelerates.
• The oil bill. The single biggest swing factor in the current account: Brent · today. Cheap crude is a rupee subsidy; expensive crude is a rupee tax with a CPI echo.
• Flows & the buffer. FDI + remittances fund a structural trade gap; FII flows set the tempo. Reserves of $·bn (≈· months of imports) are the smoothing budget — watch them fall to know the RBI is spending it.

🤖 The AI Boom, From India's Seat · MACROINTEL editorial · Jul 2026 · figures auto-refresh

The AI capital-expenditure cycle is the largest concentrated investment wave since the shale boom — chips, then the power to run them, then the buildings to house them. For a macro trader the question is never whether the technology is real; it is where we are in the capex-to-cashflow gap. Booms of this shape run until the marginal dollar of investment stops finding a marginal dollar of revenue expectation — and markets usually test that boundary violently once or twice before it truly binds.

India sits in this trade three ways, and they do not point the same direction.

First, IT services — the crowded question. Generative AI simultaneously deflates the old business (fewer billable seats per unit of work) and creates the new one (every enterprise on earth needs integration, data plumbing, governance). The market has been paying for the threat and doubting the opportunity — Nifty IT is ·% over the past month against a Nifty at ·. The honest read: seat-price deflation is certain, the integration super-cycle is probable, and the two arrive on different clocks. That argues for selectivity — names that can show AI deal flow in the order book, not the sector as a block.

Second, the picks-and-shovels at home. Data centres are a power story before they are a technology story, and India's build-out maps onto companies we already track: power generation and grid capex, capital goods, select real estate. This is the quieter, longer leg — it depends on domestic execution, not on Nasdaq multiples, which also makes it the leg that survives an AI-sentiment winter.

Third, the flows channel — the one nobody prices until it bites. AI-led US exceptionalism keeps the dollar bid and keeps global equity allocations tilted toward the US; every incremental FII dollar parked in the AI complex is a dollar not allocated to EM. A capex-digestion phase in the US would cut both ways for India: painful for IT and for risk appetite in the first move, supportive later if it breaks US exceptionalism and sends the dollar — and allocations — our way.

Positioning frame: barbell, not bet. Selective IT (proof-of-deal-flow) against overweight domestic AI-infrastructure beneficiaries; treat a correlation spike between Nasdaq, the dollar and Nifty IT as the tell that the digestion phase has started. Invalidation for the infrastructure leg is domestic: power capex announcements slowing, not American earnings calls.

💵 How the Dollar Is Valued — and India's Long Game · MACROINTEL editorial · Jul 2026 · figures auto-refresh

The dollar (DXY ·) has no fair value in any horizon a trader lives in. What it has is three regimes, and knowing which one you are in matters more than any model output.

Regime one: differentials rule. Most of the time the dollar is priced off rate and growth gaps — capital chases the best real yield, and when US real rates outrun the world, the dollar rises whatever the textbooks say about deficits. The tell that this regime is breaking: the dollar falling while its yield advantage holds. That divergence is the market quietly charging a fiscal or political risk premium — and it is the single most information-dense signal in FX.

Regime two: the smile. The dollar strengthens at both extremes — when the US is exceptional, and when the world is on fire and everyone hides in the reserve currency. It weakens only in the middle: US growth mediocre, rest-of-world improving. Long-dollar and short-dollar cases are therefore never symmetric bets on America; they are bets on where on the smile the world sits.

Regime three: the slow erosion. Twin deficits argue against the dollar over decades; the privileges of reserve status — trade invoicing, Treasury-market depth, network effects — mean the erosion is glacial, not sudden. The visible hedge is official-sector gold accumulation, which has been persistent across EM central banks, the RBI included. Both the "dollar is doomed" and "dollar is forever" camps overstate; the tradeable truth is the pace, and the pace is slow.

What this means for India, structurally. The rupee (USD/INR ·) inherits a mild depreciation drift from the inflation differential regardless of the dollar regime — flat USD/INR is real appreciation, and the RBI treats the drift as a feature, not a failure. A strong-dollar cycle imports tightening: FII outflow pressure, a costlier oil bill (Brent ·), reserves spent smoothing (buffer: $·bn). A weak-dollar cycle is India's tailwind scenario: EM allocation bid, easier RBI arithmetic, and gold in rupees — the household's oldest macro trade — still working through the currency leg.

Positioning frame: the dollar cycle is India's discount-rate weather. Constructive-India positions deserve full size only when the dollar is mid-smile or weakening; in a crisis-dollar spike, the first move is always to respect it — cut beta, keep the exporter hedge, and let the reserve buffer do its job before buying the fear.

📄 THE ECONOMIST'S READ — DEEP DIVE
India at 7.8%: Late-Cycle Reflation with an External Question Attached
The strongest quarter in five arrived in the same fortnight the disinflation crutch, the FCNR window and the dovish central bank all went away. That is not stagflation and it is not goldilocks. It is the part of the cycle where the macro word decides what the share screen does next.

On 31 August the national accounts printed 7.8% real growth for April–June, up from 6.9% a year earlier, with nominal GDP at 10.3% and gross value added at 8.2%. The composition matters more than the headline: manufacturing grew 9.2%, construction 7.7%, and financial, real-estate and professional services 12.1% — a broad domestic-cyclical acceleration — while mining contracted 2.4% and agriculture slowed to 3.6%. Two days earlier the monthly industrial production index had softened from 7.3% to 6.7%, and that single wobble was enough to make this terminal's regime engine queue a stagflation flip. The engine was reading one noisy monthly series because that was the only growth input wired into it. This build fixes that: the growth axis is now anchored on the quarterly print against the RBI's own full-year projection (6.7%, raised in August from 6.6%), with IIP and GST momentum read as the "at the margin" flag. On that basis growth is 1.1 points above what the central bank expected and cooling slightly at the margin — REFLATION, not STAGFLATION, and the difference is not academic, because the trade planner reads that word and rewrites every stop, target and share count from it.

Growth is above the central bank's own expectation, and broad

The full year FY26 closed at 7.7% real (provisional, 5 June) against 7.1% the year before; the last four quarters ran 8.3, 8.0, 7.8 and now 7.8. GST collections for August were ₹1.99 lakh crore, up 14.8% year-on-year, a touch slower than July's 15.4% on a sharp rise in refunds. Bank credit is growing 18.3% and deposits 14.7% (fortnight to 15 August) — the deposit figure this page printed as 2.1% for three weeks was a parser reading a section number; the bug is fixed and the true gap between credit and deposits is 3.6 points, not sixteen. July merchandise exports were a record $44.24bn, up 19.6%, with the goods deficit at $32.0bn and — the leg this page never showed until now — a $16.95bn services surplus that brings the overall deficit to $15.0bn.

Inflation: the disinflation is over, and the arithmetic says so before the data does

Headline CPI printed 4.45% for July (food 5.52%), the fourth straight acceleration off March's 3.16% low. Wholesale prices ran 9.78% with fuel and power at 20.05% and manufactured products at 8.29% — a producer–consumer wedge of more than five points that is being absorbed somewhere between the factory and the shelf. Three things now push the same way. Brent is $92 and up a third on the year on renewed US–Iran hostilities and tankers hit in the Strait of Hormuz; the RBI's own research puts the pass-through at roughly 20 basis points of headline inflation per 10% move in crude. The GST 2.0 rate cuts of 22 September 2025, which SBI Research estimated shaved 25–35 basis points off CPI, leave the year-on-year base from the September print onward. And the RBI's August projection has headline inflation at 4.7% in the September quarter and 5.9% in the December quarter. Against a 5.25% repo, that is a negative real policy rate by year-end unless something moves. August CPI lands on 12 September at 10:30; the nowcast panel on the macro tab is judged against it in public.

The RBI held — and then told you which way it is leaning

The Monetary Policy Committee met on 3–5 August and left the repo rate at 5.25% with a neutral stance, the fourth consecutive hold after 125 basis points of cuts across 2025. The minutes, published on 19 August, are the more important document. Deputy Governor Poonam Gupta said the scope for further cuts has ended and that "a case for a hike may emerge during the course of the year"; Governor Sanjay Malhotra said that "any evidence of these risks materialising may need policy tightening." Overnight money still trades below the repo — the weighted call rate was 5.18% on 31 August — but that is surplus liquidity from the dollars the RBI bought, not a market pricing cuts. The swap curve agrees: the FBIL MIBOR-OIS fixings as of 25 August put the one-year at 5.89% — 64 basis points over the repo, about two and a half quarter-point hikes priced on average over the year — and the 364-day Treasury bill cut-off at 5.80% says the same thing in the cash market. The direction of the next move is read from the nowcast, the minutes and the curve, and all three agree. Next decision: 5–7 October.

The external position: a record built on a window that has just closed

Foreign exchange reserves stand at $·bn, a record, after a $12.4bn jump in the week to 21 August. The source is not the current account. On 8 June the RBI opened a concessional dollar–rupee swap facility for FCNR(B) deposits, external commercial borrowings and overseas foreign-currency borrowings, bearing banks' hedging costs so they could pay up for diaspora money; by 21 August it had drawn $65.4bn in FCNR(B) deposits and $72.8bn in all, at a carry cost one estimate puts near $5.7bn a year. It is why the rupee recovered from its May low, why banks could retire costly wholesale deposits, and why the current account for April–June was a manageable 0.5% of GDP. The FCNR(B) leg closed on 31 August. The ECB and OFCB legs stay open to December, but the largest single support under the rupee stopped two days before this essay was written, with Brent at $92 and a Federal Reserve leaning into a hike. The external ledger on the reserves tab now shows every leg with its own date so that the one that went to zero is visible.

The global backdrop turned in a week

Chair Warsh used Jackson Hole to say the Fed "has work to do" on prices, and market-implied odds of a 25-basis-point hike at the 15–16 September meeting moved from around 40% to roughly two-thirds inside a week. The ten-year Treasury is at 4.78%, its highest since January 2025, and the thirty-year at 5.28% even after the Treasury expanded its buybacks. Gold at $4,432 is up 31% on the year but down 2.8% on the week as the rate repricing finally bit a metal that had been trading on the war. For India this is the classic squeeze: higher dollar rates, a higher oil bill, and a domestic central bank that would rather not be seen easing into it. The Shocks tab now prices a single Fed hike as the base case; the tail it keeps is a 2022-style cycle.

How macro becomes micro — the transmission, step by step

A regime is not a mood; it is a repricing of cash flows, and on this terminal it is wired. The releases enter through the ministry wire; growth and inflation momentum become a quadrant; the quadrant reaches the regime strip, the positioning note, the sector playbook, the movers' verdicts and the trade planner; a second, independent regime read off the weekly tape tilts the short-horizon share ranking by up to eight points, gated at two sigma; and the planner reads the macro word directly — a STAGFLATION or DISINFLATION label widens every stop by 15%, caps reward-to-risk at 1.5 and cuts the share count accordingly. Get the macro word right and the stop, the target, the size and the sector queue follow from it; get it wrong and they follow anyway. That is why the growth axis was rebuilt before anything else in this build, and why every panel on the macro tab now says which released number it is reading.

What this regime favours — and what the model actually holds

Two independent voices answer here, on purpose. The framework says Reflation favours the cyclical core — the revenue lines that grow with nominal GDP at 10%: credit, cement, freight, capex orders — and disfavours long duration and the rate-sensitives that were bought for cuts that are no longer coming. The model answers from price history alone and is deliberately not told the regime: its long-term composite ranks by trend and quality, its short-horizon screen by momentum and volatility, and the regime is allowed to reorder the queue but never to change a verdict. Where the two disagree — as they do today on banks, which the framework likes for the NIM and the tape has been selling — the disagreement is the information. The sector character lines on the micro tab were rewritten in this build so that none of them asserts an easing regime as fact; each names the condition and the live qualifier says whether it holds.

Gold, in rupees

Gold's macro driver is not inflation itself but the real rate — what a rupee of safety earns after inflation. Real repo at roughly 0.8% and heading toward zero on the RBI's own projection is the domestic argument for the metal; the dollar rate repricing is the argument against it, and the week's price action says the second is winning for now. The domestic chain on the commodities tab is anchored to the 31 August IBJA fix (₹155,835 per ten grams for fine gold, silver ₹237,199 a kilo) with the wedge to import parity stored on the same day's spot, and it is hand-refreshed until a door to the fix lands on the runner.

Commodities — the regime's favourite asset, and India's oldest tax

Globally, Reflation is the commodity-friendly quadrant: demand accelerating into supply that cannot respond quickly. For an importer it is a tax. At 1.79 billion barrels a year, every $10 on Brent is $18bn on the import bill — about a fifth of a month's goods deficit — and 20 basis points on headline CPI on the RBI's estimate. The oil panel on the commodities tab runs that arithmetic live against the current price; the sector-inflation cards on the macro tab say who pays it (cement, paints, aviation) and who collects it (upstream, coal, refiners).

What would change the call

Honesty about a regime call means naming its tripwires in advance. First: the August CPI print on 12 September. A print at or above 4.7% confirms the nowcast and the RBI's path; a print back under 4.3% would say the oil and base-effect arithmetic is being offset by food, and the inflation axis loses its momentum basis. Second: the September FOMC on the 15th–16th. A hike with hawkish guidance is the external squeeze in full; a hold with the ten-year still above 4.7% is the more uncomfortable version. Third: the rupee without the window. The first weekly reserve prints after 31 August tell you whether the ECB/OFCB legs and portfolio flows replace what FCNR(B) was providing; a drawdown with the rupee under pressure is the scenario the scorecard says to respect most. Fourth: 5–7 October. The minutes have told you the direction; the meeting tells you the timing.

The verdict

Late-cycle reflation with an external funding question attached. Growth is above the central bank's expectation and broad; inflation is rising toward a path the central bank itself has published; the policy rate is on hold with the next move more likely up than down; and the external cushion that made all of it comfortable was a temporary facility that has just ended. For the book that means: own the nominal-growth beneficiaries, not the duration; treat rate-sensitives as a funding-cost story until the curve says otherwise; keep the oil importers on a short leash while Hormuz is live; and size everything off the regime the engine now reads from the national accounts rather than from one month of industrial production. Constructive, conditional, and — this time — measured.
