📉 INDIA OIS & MIBOR — WHAT THE MARKET PRICES FOR THE RBI
This is India's version of "Fed Funds futures" — the MIBOR-OIS curve prices exactly what the market expects the RBI to do. MIBOR (Mumbai Interbank Offered Rate) is the overnight benchmark; the OIS (Overnight Indexed Swap) curve built on it reveals the priced path of rate cuts. Reading this curve tells you whether the bond market agrees with our regime call — and whether rate-sensitive sectors (banks, NBFCs, autos, real estate) have a tailwind coming.
1 · The Policy Corridor & Benchmarks (latest)
📐 "Isn't MIBOR around 7.5%?" — Why it structurally can't be, in June 2026
A common reflex is to remember MIBOR near 7%+. That was true in 2023–24, when the repo rate was 6.50%. But the RBI has cut three times since (Dec 2025 and Feb 2026 among them), and the repo is now 5.25% (held at the June 3–5 MPC, neutral stance). MIBOR moves with the repo, so it has come down too.

The hard ceiling: overnight MIBOR is the rate banks lend each other unsecured, and it is boxed inside the RBI's policy corridor:
  MSF (ceiling) ........ 5.50% ← no bank borrows interbank above this; it'd use RBI instead
  Repo rate ........... 5.25% ← the policy anchor
  SDF (floor) ......... 5.00% ← no bank lends below this; it'd park at RBI instead
  Overnight MIBOR ..... ~5.3–5.9% (sits just around the corridor)
A 7.5% overnight MIBOR would be 200 bps above the MSF ceiling — impossible, because any bank could just borrow from the RBI at 5.50% instead of paying 7.50% to another bank. The corridor is a hard cap.

What about the 3-month rate in this terminal? It is the 3-month money-market rate (OIS-implied) — a term rate, which sits above overnight because it carries term premium. Overnight ≈ ~5.16%, 3-month ≈ 5.78%. (An earlier note here cited a 6.63% "term MIBOR" from an 18-June snapshot — that constant went stale and has been retired.) Neither is anywhere near 7.5%.

Bottom line: with repo at 5.25%, MIBOR in the 5.3% (overnight) to 6.6% (3-month) range is exactly where it should be. 7.5% would require a repo rate near 7% — which India hasn't seen since 2018, or briefly in the 2023 tightening peak (repo 6.50%, term MIBOR ~7%). The number isn't wrong; it's just lower than it was two years ago because the RBI has eased.

5 · OIS Rates Table & the Swap Spread (OIS vs G-Sec)
The OIS rate is the market's prediction of the average overnight rate over each tenor — not the current repo. The OIS curve is the "cleaner" risk-free curve; the G-Sec curve carries supply/demand noise. Their difference is the swap spread, which tells you about bond supply pressure and term premium.

What the swap spread says: the 1Y OIS sits +33bp over repo and +47bp over the overnight call — hikes priced at the front. The signal: the front-end OIS pricing easing is the cleaner read on RBI policy; the wide long-end swap spread is fiscal supply, not a changed rate view. For a trader: the OIS front-end is where to read RBI intent; the G-Sec long end is where supply/demand noise lives. (Per the OIS-vs-bond-curve distinction: OIS is the "truer" risk-free expectation curve, free of supply distortions.)

🛡️ FOREX RESERVES — INDIA'S WAR CHEST (FULLY LIVE)
Nothing on this tab is hand-typed. The headline number scrapes from the RBI data portal every pipeline pass, the history comes from the FRED/IMF monthly series, and every metric below is computed from those two feeds at render time.
History — monthly (FRED / IMF, ex-gold) with weekly RBI prints overlaid
History chart fills after the next pipeline pass fetches the FRED series.
What this number actually is. The RBI's stock of foreign-currency assets, gold, SDRs and the IMF position — the buffer that lets a managed-float currency be managed. It is not a savings account; it is ammunition: sold to slow rupee depreciation, accumulated when flows are friendly.

How to read the moves. Reserves rising = the RBI is buying dollars (absorbing inflows, keeping the rupee competitive). Reserves falling = the RBI is defending — spending the buffer to smooth depreciation, or valuation effects (a stronger dollar and softer gold mark the non-dollar assets down without a single intervention). Fast drawdowns during INR pressure are the market's best evidence of how hard the RBI is leaning.

Why traders care. The buffer sets how long the RBI can keep USD/INR orderly. A fat buffer means step-moves are chosen, not forced; a thinning one means the currency, rates or both must eventually do the adjusting — which is a regime input, not a footnote.
Import cover divides the reserves by the monthly goods import bill: ·. Component split (FCA / gold / SDR / IMF) publishes in the RBI's Weekly Statistical Supplement each Friday — that feed isn't wired yet, so we show the live totals rather than a stale breakdown. Adequacy thresholds shown are the standard rules of thumb (3m minimal, 6-8m comfortable, 10m+ fortress), applied mechanically.

💵 THE DOLLAR — how it's valued, and why India cares
There is no fair-value anchor for the dollar in the short run — only regimes. Three lenses do most of the work:

1 · Rate & growth differentials. Capital chases the highest risk-adjusted real yield. When US real rates and growth outrun the rest of G10, the dollar gets bid regardless of valuation. But differentials can decouple for months when fiscal or political risk premia take over — which is exactly the information: when the dollar falls despite a yield advantage, the market is charging the US a risk premium.

2 · The dollar smile. The dollar strengthens at both extremes — exceptional US growth (money chases US assets) and global crisis (money hides in the reserve currency). It weakens in the middle: mediocre US growth with the rest of the world improving. Placing today on the smile tells you more than any single indicator.

3 · The structural ledger. Twin deficits argue for a weaker dollar over decades; reserve-currency privilege (invoicing, Treasury demand, network effects) argues it erodes only slowly. Central-bank gold accumulation is the visible hedge against that erosion — diversification is real, but glacial. Both things are true; the trade is in the pace.

🤖 The AI Boom, From India's Seat · MACROINTEL editorial · Jul 2026 · figures auto-refresh

The AI capital-expenditure cycle is the largest concentrated investment wave since the shale boom — chips, then the power to run them, then the buildings to house them. For a macro trader the question is never whether the technology is real; it is where we are in the capex-to-cashflow gap. Booms of this shape run until the marginal dollar of investment stops finding a marginal dollar of revenue expectation — and markets usually test that boundary violently once or twice before it truly binds.

India sits in this trade three ways, and they do not point the same direction.

First, IT services — the crowded question. Generative AI simultaneously deflates the old business (fewer billable seats per unit of work) and creates the new one (every enterprise on earth needs integration, data plumbing, governance). The market has been paying for the threat and doubting the opportunity — Nifty IT is ·% over the past month against a Nifty at ·. The honest read: seat-price deflation is certain, the integration super-cycle is probable, and the two arrive on different clocks. That argues for selectivity — names that can show AI deal flow in the order book, not the sector as a block.

Second, the picks-and-shovels at home. Data centres are a power story before they are a technology story, and India's build-out maps onto companies we already track: power generation and grid capex, capital goods, select real estate. This is the quieter, longer leg — it depends on domestic execution, not on Nasdaq multiples, which also makes it the leg that survives an AI-sentiment winter.

Third, the flows channel — the one nobody prices until it bites. 💵 How the Dollar Is Valued — and India's Long Game · MACROINTEL editorial · Jul 2026 · figures auto-refresh

The dollar (DXY ·) has no fair value in any horizon a trader lives in. What it has is three regimes, and knowing which one you are in matters more than any model output.

Regime one: differentials rule. Most of the time the dollar is priced off rate and growth gaps — capital chases the best real yield, and when US real rates outrun the world, the dollar rises whatever the textbooks say about deficits. The tell that this regime is breaking: the dollar falling while its yield advantage holds. That divergence is the market quietly charging a fiscal or political risk premium — and it is the single most information-dense signal in FX.

Regime two: the smile. The dollar strengthens at both extremes — when the US is exceptional, and when the world is on fire and everyone hides in the reserve currency. It weakens only in the middle: US growth mediocre, rest-of-world improving. Long-dollar and short-dollar cases are therefore never symmetric bets on America; they are bets on where on the smile the world sits.

Regime three: the slow erosion. Twin deficits argue against the dollar over decades; the privileges of reserve status — trade invoicing, Treasury-market depth, network effects — mean the erosion is glacial, not sudden. The visible hedge is official-sector gold accumulation, which has been persistent across EM central banks, the RBI included. Both the "dollar is doomed" and "dollar is forever" camps overstate; the tradeable truth is the pace, and the pace is slow.

What this means for India, structurally. The rupee (USD/INR ·) inherits a mild depreciation drift from the inflation differential regardless of the dollar regime — flat USD/INR is real appreciation, and the RBI treats the drift as a feature, not a failure. A strong-dollar cycle imports tightening: FII outflow pressure, a costlier oil bill (Brent ·), reserves spent smoothing (buffer: $·bn). A weak-dollar cycle is India's tailwind scenario: EM allocation bid, easier RBI arithmetic, and gold in rupees — the household's oldest macro trade — still working through the currency leg.

Positioning frame: the dollar cycle is India's discount-rate weather. Constructive-India positions deserve full size only when the dollar is mid-smile or weakening; in a crisis-dollar spike, the first move is always to respect it — cut beta, keep the exporter hedge, and let the reserve buffer do its job before buying the fear.

📄 THE ECONOMIST'S READ — DEEP DIVE
India at the Turn: Reflation, Confirmed Three Ways
The June coin-flip resolved. Growth is accelerating into inflation that has just crossed the target, the RBI held exactly as the curve said it would, and the producer-price pipeline is the number to argue about now.

Six weeks ago the honest characterisation of this economy was a coin-flip: everything hinged on whether June CPI printed above or below 4%. It printed 4.38%, above, and rising from 3.93% — and in the same fortnight industrial production accelerated to a 23-month high and GST collections set a July record (April 2026's ₹2.42 lakh cr remains the all-time month). That combination has a name. Growth rising with inflation rising is Reflation, and for the first time all three of this terminal's methods say it at once: the release-momentum engine, the level grid, and the retrained classifier at 93%. When three methods with different failure modes agree, the read deserves respect; when they disagree, this page now says so out loud rather than letting you find out later.

Growth is accelerating, and it is broad

Industrial production rose 7.3% year-on-year in June against 5.1% in May — the strongest print in nearly two years, led by manufacturing, capital goods and electricity rather than a single lucky category. The corroborating witness is GST: July gross collections of ₹2.11 lakh crore, up 15.4% on the year — the highest July on record (April 2026 holds the all-time month at ₹2.42 lakh cr) — with import collections surging — the broadest high-frequency read on nominal activity India publishes, and it is saying the same thing as the factory data.

Inflation crossed the target — and the pipeline says there is more behind it

The CPI path over six prints tells the story better than any single number: the trail bottomed in the mid-3s and has climbed for three consecutive prints to 4.38%, with food at 5.32% doing more than its share. On its own that is a young breach of the target, not a crisis. What sharpens it is the producer side: wholesale inflation at 9.87% — 5.5 points above CPI — with the fuel and power group running at 27.41%. A wedge that wide means costs are being absorbed between the factory gate and the shelf. Someone is paying it: the sector panel on the macro tab names who, with the arithmetic shown, and the short version is that energy-intensive producers are squeezed, upstream energy is collecting, and manufacturers still hold pricing power over the consumer.

The RBI held — exactly as the curve said it would

The Monetary Policy Committee met on 4-6 August and left the repo rate at 5.25% with a neutral stance. Nobody watching the swap curve was surprised: one-year OIS sits about a quarter-point below the overnight fixing — roughly one cut priced over a year, with the priced path bottoming near the two-year point. Real repo, the policy rate net of CPI, is now under 1% and shrinking as inflation rises: policy is drifting looser without the RBI lifting a finger, which is precisely why a hold was the coherent choice for a committee watching a 4-handle CPI with a 27% fuel WPI behind it. The external position is the cushion under all of it

Foreign exchange reserves stand at $·bn (weekly RBI print, dated on the reserves tab), within sight of the all-time high — a war chest that lets the RBI smooth the rupee (USD/INR ·) rather than defend it. Brent at $· keeps the import bill inside the comfort band, though the rupee price of crude on the macro tab is the version of that number India actually experiences. Nothing in the external accounts currently argues against the domestic read.

How macro becomes micro — the transmission, step by step

A regime is not a mood; it is a repricing of cash flows, and it reaches individual shares through four channels. Earnings: reflation means nominal GDP is accelerating, and cyclical revenue lines (credit, cement volumes, freight, capex orders) accelerate with it while defensive revenue lines merely hold. Rates: rising inflation expectations raise the discount rate, which taxes long-duration equity (profits far in the future — expensive growth, loss-makers) more than short-duration equity (profits now — value, cyclicals). Margins: the wedge decides who keeps the growth — the sector panel above prices this channel from the actual WPI groups. Flows: the liquidity backdrop and FII behaviour set the beta multiplier on all of it. That is the whole chain: regime → rates → margins → earnings revisions → the tape. The REGIME → MICRO panel on the shares tab is this argument run on history rather than prose.

What this regime favours — and what the model actually holds

Two independent voices answer here, on purpose. The framework says Reflation favours the cyclical core — banks and lenders, industrials and capital goods, metals, autos, real-economy infrastructure — funded by underweights in long-duration growth and bond-proxies, with short duration on the rates side. The model (price-based trend and quality, no macro opinion in it) publishes its own top holdings daily. Where the two agree, conviction compounds; where a model pick sits outside the favoured set, that is trend fighting macro — size it accordingly. Reflation compresses that number from both ends: the RBI is holding the nominal rate while CPI climbs, so real repo is under 1% and shrinking, which lowers the cost of holding the zero-yield asset. Commodities — the regime's favourite asset, and India's oldest tax

Globally, Reflation is the commodity-friendly quadrant: demand is accelerating into supply that cannot respond quickly. But India sits on the other side of that trade — a structural importer of energy, so rising commodities are simultaneously a boon to the producers listed on this exchange (upstream energy, coal, metals — the sector panel already prices this) and a tax on everyone else through the import bill and, with a lag, through CPI itself. What would change the call

Honesty about a regime call means naming its tripwires in advance. First: the July CPI print, due around 12 August. A soft print would flip the inflation axis and propose Goldilocks — growth up, inflation cooling, the friendliest quadrant there is. The engine will not whipsaw on it: a proposed flip now needs either corroboration from wholesale prices or a second confirming release, and until then the strip shows it as pending. Second: the fuel WPI. 27.41% has base effects in it; if it normalises fast, the wedge closes benignly and the squeeze verdicts soften. Third: the market cross-check. If the tape's own cycle read deteriorates while the releases stay hot, believe the divergence is telling you something about what is already priced. This page will show all three the day they move.

The AI exposure, in one honest paragraph

India's most AI-exposed sector cuts both ways: the IT services franchise earns dollars maintaining exactly the workflows the technology is learning to compress, which is why the long-term model has kept the sector near the bottom of its ranks even while the index made highs — and at the same time, the domestic-demand core of this market (banks, cement, autos, power, consumer) is about as insulated from that compression as any equity complex in the world. A Reflation regime happens to favour precisely the insulated half. That is a coincidence worth noticing, not a thesis.

The verdict

Constructive, conditional, and no longer a coin-flip: the economy is reflating, the policy rate is on hold with the market pricing a slow drift lower, the external position is a cushion, and the one number that could rewrite the story lands within the week. The book that fits this page is the one the strip has been naming — cyclicals over defensives, short duration over long, with the invalidation levels written next to every position. When the regime changes, this page will say so before it feels obvious.
